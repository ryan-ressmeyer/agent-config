# Structural Editing

Structural editing operates on the document's ideas and logical architecture rather than sentence polish. The working unit may be a claim, paragraph function, section, transition, or proposed sequence.

## Reverse outlining

Use a reverse outline when the existing structure is difficult to see or the author asks to assess narrative flow.

- Extract claims, not topics. “Orientation tuning sharpens after stimulus onset” is a claim; “temporal dynamics” is a topic.
- Match granularity to the agreed scope. Use paragraph-level claims for a section and section-level claims for a whole manuscript unless finer detail is needed to expose the problem.
- Present the outline in chat. A flat sequence is usually best for testing whether each claim prepares the next, but preserve grouping when the author needs to reason about section boundaries.
- Check logical progression, missing premises, redundancy, ordering, balance, delayed payoff, and whether a promised question remains active until answered.

A reverse outline is a tool for conversation, not a required deliverable. Do not save it unless the author asks.

## Collaborative structural loop

1. Show the relevant claim sequence or structural diagnosis.
2. Identify the highest-leverage issue or one coherent cluster of issues.
3. Propose a concrete revised sequence, cut, merge, or addition.
4. Resolve that decision with the author before moving to the next structural issue.

Keep structural alternatives concrete. Show the resulting sequence or paragraph functions rather than saying only that material should be “reorganized.” Preserve valuable content unless the author chooses to cut it.

Do not polish sentences while the argument is still moving. If sentence-level wording becomes the active concern, tell the author that the task has crossed into line editing and follow their choice about switching levels.
