# Manuscript Style

Read this reference when drafting or editing scientific manuscripts. It supplements the shared rules in `../SKILL.md`. Use `scientific-claims-reference` for evidence attribution, causal scope, uncertainty, generalization, and consensus claims.

## Priorities

Preserve scientific meaning before improving style. Give the reader the definitions, premises, methods, qualifications, and quantitative evidence needed to follow the argument. Then improve logical structure and concision. Never delete a necessary condition or limitation merely to shorten a sentence.

## Sentence construction

- Put the grammatical subject and main verb early.
- Avoid long introductory modifiers that force readers to reinterpret the opening after reaching the main clause.
- Keep modifiers near the words they qualify.
- Prefer one main action or claim per sentence. Split a sentence that combines the question, method, result, and interpretation.
- Prefer sentences that can be understood in one reading. Sentence length is not itself the target.
- Use a longer sentence when it expresses a comparison, dependency, or qualification more clearly than several short sentences would.
- Vary sentence length without stacking fragments or producing a sequence of equally weighted short statements.

### Colons

Do not use a colon to connect a complete claim to its explanation, consequence, qualification, interpretation, or elaboration. This construction turns the material after the colon into an appended clause and often conceals a run-on sentence.

Avoid constructions such as these.

> The analysis has a structural problem: movement and retinal input covary.

> One rule applies: split the claim and explanation into separate sentences.

State the relationship directly instead.

> Movement and retinal input covary, which creates a structural problem for the analysis.

> Split the claim and explanation into separate sentences.

Use a colon only when it is difficult to avoid without reducing clarity. Legitimate cases include a compact list introduced by a complete clause, a formal definition, a ratio, a clock time, or comparable technical notation. Prefer an integrated sentence when a list is short.

### Em dashes

Em dashes are acceptable for a genuine parenthetical interruption or apposition when commas or parentheses would obscure the sentence. Do not use them for dramatic pivots, false contrasts, or repeated emphasis. Avoid stacking more than one parenthetical interruption in a sentence.

## Paragraph architecture

- Give each paragraph one main idea.
- Begin with a functional topic sentence that establishes the paragraph's subject or claim. Do not turn it into a slogan or pull quote.
- Arrange supporting sentences so that each follows from the previous sentence.
- Use the final sentence to state the consequence or prepare the next logical move when that connection is needed.
- Do not append a summary sentence when the evidence already completes the point.
- Split a paragraph when its sentences no longer support the same controlling idea.
- Do not place an unrelated acquisition or preprocessing detail inside a paragraph about a biological result. Keep methods, findings, and interpretation in separate paragraphs unless the methodological detail directly supports the paragraph's claim.

Reading the topic sentences in sequence should reveal the argument's progression. This is a diagnostic rather than a rigid template.

## Concision without under-explanation

Remove dispensable words, repeated premises, editorial intensifiers, and procedural narration. Preserve information that readers need to understand what was measured, under which conditions, and how the evidence supports the claim.

An occasional roadmap is appropriate when a long or technically dense section would otherwise be difficult to navigate. A roadmap must identify a real sequence or distinction. Do not use it merely to announce that text follows.

## Scientific conventions

### Tense

- Use past tense for procedures and observations from the completed study.
- Use present tense for the contents of figures and tables and for conclusions presented as currently supported.
- Keep tense stable within a passage unless the time or epistemic status changes.
- Follow journal conventions when they differ.

### Abbreviations

Minimize abbreviations coined for a single manuscript. Define one only when it recurs enough to reduce rather than increase cognitive load. Prefer the full term when an abbreviation appears only a few times or competes with an established meaning.

### Specific terminology

- Name the organism, preparation, population, cell type, or assay when `in vivo` or `in vitro` would hide information relevant to interpretation.
- Distinguish changes to DNA from amino-acid substitutions in the encoded protein.
- Use `participant` for people enrolled in research unless a more specific term is required by the study or reporting standard.
- Use sex and gender terminology that matches what the study measured. Preserve categories reported by the source and identify measurement limitations when relevant.
- Reserve `prove` for formal mathematical proof. Describe empirical evidence as supporting, contradicting, constraining, or changing confidence in a claim.

### Quantitative prose

Report the quantities needed to evaluate the result. Depending on the analysis, these may include effect sizes, uncertainty intervals, sample sizes, test statistics, exact probability values, model comparisons, and figure references. Name the statistical method when it is not already clear from nearby text.

Do not use `significant` as a substitute for reporting the effect or statistical evidence. Avoid treating a thresholded probability value as a measure of scientific importance. Apply `scientific-claims-reference` when deciding what inference the design and analysis support.
