# Scientific Claims Reference

Scientific claims should preserve the relationship between evidence, interpretation, and scope. Apply these conventions when drafting or evaluating manuscripts, paper summaries, thematic syntheses, reviews, and figure narratives.

## Evidence and attribution

- Attribute empirical findings to the studies that reported them. Prefer “Burr et al. found…” over floating constructions such as “it has been shown.”
- State what measurement supports a claim. A citation alone does not explain the evidentiary connection.
- Distinguish reported results from authors’ interpretations. “The authors concluded X” is not equivalent to “X is the case.”
- Preserve quantitative support when available: sample sizes, effect sizes, uncertainty intervals, test statistics, and figure references.
- When evidence and interpretation diverge, report both rather than silently adopting the interpretation.

## Scope and generalization

- Preserve species, preparation, population, stimulus, task, and model boundaries that matter to interpretation.
- Do not cite a result from one preparation as though it directly establishes a broader biological or behavioral claim.
- Name methodological differences when comparing studies, especially when they could explain apparently conflicting results.
- State important missing perspectives or populations when the available evidence is a limited sample of the field.

## Uncertainty and causal language

- Match certainty to the design and evidence. Correlation, prediction, mediation, necessity, sufficiency, and causation are different claims.
- Avoid “clearly,” “conclusively,” “definitive,” and similar certainty language unless a precise evidentiary reason is supplied.
- Report marginal or unstable results as such; do not promote exploratory findings into primary conclusions.
- Identify missing controls, circular analyses, or alternative explanations when they bear on the claim.

## Consensus and coverage

- A local literature database, selected review set, or theme collection is not the whole literature.
- Do not infer consensus from several mutually consistent papers. Say which papers agree and in what respects.
- Avoid “it is widely accepted,” “extensive evidence demonstrates,” and “it is now understood” unless the task includes evidence that establishes field-level agreement.
- When relevant viewpoints are absent, state the coverage gap rather than smoothing the available studies into a complete narrative.

## Historical and evaluative claims

- Do not claim a study was first, pioneering, landmark, seminal, or field-defining unless the historical claim is itself supported.
- Avoid praise words such as “striking,” “elegant,” “critical,” or “important” in place of explaining the result’s actual consequence.
- Describe how a paper changed a question, method, or inference rather than assigning prestige labels.

## Cross-study synthesis

- Organize synthesis by questions, claims, mechanisms, or methodological contrasts rather than concatenating paper summaries.
- Attribute each point of agreement or tension to the relevant studies.
- Treat disagreement as information. Do not force reconciliation when methods or outcomes genuinely conflict.
- Separate open questions raised by the evidence from speculative answers.
- Limit caveats to those that affect the interpretation under discussion; an exhaustive limitations catalogue can obscure the comparison.

## Unknowns

When the evidence available to the task cannot support a requested claim:

- identify the unsupported part precisely;
- state what evidence is available;
- name the missing paper, comparison, control, or literature coverage when known;
- ask whether the user wants to narrow the claim or gather additional evidence.
